Chapter 00

Welcome

This manual explains how Phoenix — the system this business runs its accounting, loans, savings and branch operations on — actually works. It assumes no prior accounting background, and builds up to how each screen you use turns into an entry in the general ledger.

Who this is for

Every chapter is written twice in one page. The plain-language instructions — how to submit a loan, take a deposit, raise a petty cash voucher — are always visible. Turn on Accountant view in the sidebar and each chapter also shows the exact debit/credit entries that action creates, so someone new to accounting can watch a familiar task turn into the two-sided ledger postings behind it.

How to use this manual

Read Chapters 01–02 in order first — they're the foundation everything else builds on. After that, jump straight to whichever module your role touches using the sidebar, or the search box at the top of it.

What Phoenix actually is

Phoenix is a branch-based financial system: every client, loan, savings account, bank account and voucher belongs to a specific branch, and almost everything you do posts a balanced entry into that branch's own set of accounts (its "chart of accounts"). A small number of head-office roles can see and post across every branch at once, and can produce one combined, "consolidated" set of books for the whole organisation.

Three ideas recur in almost every chapter, so it's worth meeting them here first:

  • Every transaction is double-sided. Nothing moves money without an equal and opposite entry elsewhere — Chapter 02 explains why.
  • Most approvals are maker-checker. The person who requests, the person who approves, and the person who pays out are usually required to be three different people. You'll see this pattern in loans, petty cash and bank transfers.
  • Big or unusual actions page a director. Certain events — an over-limit loan approval, a large bank transfer, a petty cash payout — send an automatic alert to a director's Telegram group, whether or not anyone asks for it.

Chapter 01

Getting Started

Your login, your branch, and what you'll actually see depend on the role you've been assigned.

Branches and "your" data

When you log in, Phoenix already knows your home branch and your role. Most roles only ever see that branch's clients, loans, savings accounts and cash — this is called own_branch scope. A handful of senior roles (see Chapter 11) have global scope and can switch between branches, or select "All Branches" to see the organisation as a whole.

There is no separate "head office" branch in the system — head office isn't a place, it's a permission. Anyone with global scope effectively gets a head-office view over every branch's books at once.

Your role decides what you can do

Every role carries two things: a scope (whose records you can see) and an approval limit (the largest amount you're allowed to approve or disburse on your own signature). Try to approve something above your limit and Phoenix blocks it and quietly pages a director instead — see Chapter 11 for the full table of roles and limits.

Finding your way around

  • Clients & Loans, Savings — day-to-day work for tellers, loan officers and recovery officers.
  • Cash & Banking, Petty Cash — where physical and bank money is tracked and moved.
  • Reports — trial balance, profit & loss, balance sheet and more, for accountants and management.
  • Income & Expenses, Inventory & Procurement — everything the business buys, holds or bills for outside of lending.
Tip

If a screen or button you expect isn't visible, it's almost always a permission, not a bug — ask your branch manager whether your role or scope needs adjusting.

Chapter 02

Accounting Fundamentals

Everything downstream in this manual — loans, savings, petty cash, reports — is really just this chapter applied to a specific situation. It's worth reading slowly once.

Double-entry: every entry has two sides

Money never moves in Phoenix without two things happening at once: one account is debited, another is credited, for the exact same amount. A loan disbursement isn't just "money went out" — it's "Cash went down and Loan Receivable went up, by the same ₦50,000." This pairing is what lets the books always prove themselves: if you add up every debit and every credit in the system, they must be equal.

A common misconception: debit doesn't mean "subtract" and credit doesn't mean "add." Which one increases a balance depends on the type of account:

Account typeA debit…A credit…Everyday example
Asset (things you own)increasesdecreasesCash, Bank, Loan Receivable, Inventory
Liability (things you owe)decreasesincreasesCustomer Savings Deposits, Accounts Payable
EquitydecreasesincreasesOwner's capital, retained earnings
IncomedecreasesincreasesInterest Income, Penalty Income, Fee Income
ExpenseincreasesdecreasesRent, Utilities, Salaries

Reading a Phoenix ledger entry

Throughout this manual, ledger postings are shown in this format — the same shape as a traditional cash book, just typed:

AccountDebit (₦)Credit (₦)
Cash at Teller50,000
Loan Receivable — LN-104250,000

Read this as: cash went up by ₦50,000 (a debit to an asset), and the client's loan balance owed to the branch went down by ₦50,000 (a credit to an asset) — a repayment coming in.

The Chart of Accounts

Every branch has its own full set of accounts, numbered by type in blocks: 1000s Assets, 2000s Liabilities, 3000s Equity, 4000s Income, 5000s Expenses. Most accounts you'll never touch directly: a "parent" account like 1100 — Cash and Cash Equivalents is just a heading, while its "child" accounts underneath — 1101 Cash on Hand, 1102 Petty Cash, 1104 First City Bank — Current — are the ones that actually receive postings.

A special kind of child account, a sub-ledger, is auto-created and linked one-to-one to a single real-world thing: one per loan, one per savings account, one per till/cashier. This is how the system can tell you "LN-1042 owes ₦38,000" instead of just "Loans Receivable in total is ₦14.2m" — and it's also why reports hide these individual sub-ledger rows by default (Chapter 10) while still counting them correctly in the parent total.

Journal entries and the General Ledger

Every posting — whether typed by hand as a journal voucher or generated automatically by disbursing a loan — is one transaction with two or more lines, each hitting one account as a debit or a credit. A transaction can only be saved if its debits and credits are exactly equal; Phoenix will not let an unbalanced entry through. Once a transaction is approved, it is not edited — a mistake is corrected by reversing it (posting an equal and opposite entry that cancels it out, keeping a full trail of what happened) rather than deleting it.

Every transaction is stamped with a series — a short code like LNDIS for a loan disbursement or SVDEP for a savings deposit — which is really just which "book" the entry was filed under, similar to a paper ledger having a separate Cash Book, Sales Day Book and Purchases Day Book.

Trial balance and the financial statements

A trial balance is simply a list of every account and its balance on a given date, with total debits proving equal to total credits. From that same underlying data, Phoenix can also produce a Profit & Loss statement (income minus expenses, over a period) and a Balance Sheet (assets, liabilities and equity, at a point in time). Chapter 10 covers each report in detail.

Why this matters

Once you can read a two-line Dr/Cr entry, you can read every "Accountant view" box in this manual — they're all built from exactly this pattern, just with different accounts.

Chapter 03

Clients & Loans

From onboarding a client through to a loan being fully paid off.

Onboarding a client

A client record captures identity, contact and next-of-kin details, an ID type and number, and — required and validated — their NIN and BVN. Clients are classified by how they contribute: Daily, Weekly, Monthly contributor, or Prospect, and each is assigned a credit/loan officer and an account manager. A client also has a KYC status (pending → submitted → verified) tracked for compliance record-keeping.

Someone who guarantees a loan without being a full client themselves is recorded as a Guarantor — they can later be converted into a full client if they take a loan of their own.

Loan products

Each loan product fixes the rules a loan under it must follow: interest method (flat-rate or reducing balance), minimum/maximum amount and term, allowed repayment frequency (daily, weekly, biweekly, monthly, quarterly), processing fee, insurance, and a late-payment penalty with its own grace period. A product can also require collateral and a minimum number of guarantors before a loan against it can be approved.

Application and approval

Pending→Approved→Disbursed→Active→Paid Off

A loan officer submits the application; approval is separate step performed by someone else — the system will not let the person who created the loan also approve it. If the product requires guarantors, approval is blocked until enough are attached.

Disbursement is its own three-person approval

Approving a loan does not release funds. Disbursement is a distinct workflow: a branch manager or supervisor approves the disbursement request, and then a third person — finance or a teller — actually pays it out. Requester, approver and disburser must be three different people.

  • Loan officer submits the loan application.
  • A different, authorised person approves the loan.
  • A branch manager/supervisor approves the disbursement request.
  • Finance/teller executes the disbursement, choosing the cash till or bank account funds are paid from.
    • 📒 In the ledger — disbursing ₦50,000LNDIS
    AccountDebitCredit
    Loan Receivable — LN-104250,000
    Cash / Bank (disbursing account)50,000

    By default, Phoenix also recognises the loan's full scheduled interest as income immediately at disbursement, matching how this business has always run its books — it adds the interest onto the Loan Receivable debit and credits Interest Income for the same amount, in the same entry. A slower, "as-it's-earned" alternative exists for products configured for it, but immediate recognition is the default.

    The repayment schedule

    The moment a loan is disbursed, Phoenix generates its full repayment schedule: one row per installment, each with a due date and its share of principal, interest, fees and any penalty. Flat-rate products split interest evenly across every installment; reducing-balance products calculate a standard declining-balance amortisation, same as a conventional loan schedule.

    Repayments and penalties

    A missed installment doesn't just sit there — once it passes its grace period, a penalty is assessed and booked as income immediately, whether or not the client has paid anything yet. When a payment does come in, it isn't swallowed entirely by an overdue penalty: Phoenix splits the payment proportionally between the outstanding penalty and everything else, by their relative share of what's owed, so a partial payment always makes some progress on the loan itself. Within the non-penalty portion, interest is settled before principal — unless a loan has gone seriously delinquent, in which case that order flips to protect the recorded principal balance first.

    📒 In the ledger — a routine repayment

    Because interest was already recognised as income at disbursement, an on-time repayment is usually a simple two-line entry — it isn't re-crediting income a second time:

    AccountDebitCredit
    Cash at Teller12,500
    Loan Receivable — LN-104212,500
    Good to know

    Write-offs (formally closing out an unrecoverable loan) exist as a feature but are not part of how this business currently manages bad debt in practice — don't expect to see them used day to day.

    Closing a loan

    A loan moves itself to Paid Off automatically the instant a payment brings its outstanding principal, interest, fees and penalties all to zero — there's no separate "close the loan" step. If a loan had previously been flagged as defaulted and a payment leaves any balance still outstanding, it's automatically restored to Active rather than staying marked as default.

    Chapter 04

    Savings

    Every client automatically has a savings account before they ever ask for one.

    Regular Savings — created automatically

    The moment a new client record is created, Phoenix opens a Regular Savings account for them behind the scenes, with a minimum balance of at least ₦1,000 regardless of what any product configuration says. Nothing needs collecting to open it — it's simply ready for the client's first deposit.

    Other savings products

    • Daily Contribution ("Ajo") accounts — for clients expected to deposit small amounts frequently against a fixed contribution schedule.
    • Savings Cycle products, incl. Smart Savings — a fixed term (Smart Savings runs a 3-month cycle) earning interest at maturity, with a penalty if the client breaks the cycle and withdraws early.

    A client can also have savings automatically topped up: if a compulsory-savings policy is switched on for the branch, disbursing a loan automatically deposits a fixed amount straight into the client's savings out of the same funds used to disburse the loan.

    Taking a deposit

    📒 In the ledger — a cash depositSVDEP
    AccountDebitCredit
    Cash at Teller5,000
    Customer Savings — client's account5,000

    The first-deposit income sweep

    For daily-contribution products configured this way, the first deposit collected in a calendar month is treated specially: up to the amount the client has committed to contributing, it is swept straight to income rather than credited to the client's savings balance — anything collected above that committed amount is deposited normally. This mirrors how the business has always accounted for the first collection of the month on these products; it only fires once per month, and never applies to a Smart Savings-enrolled account.

    📒 In the ledger — first deposit of the month, ₦2,000 committedSV1ST
    AccountDebitCredit
    Cash at Teller2,000
    Collection Income2,000

    Withdrawals — always maker-checker

    A withdrawal is never a single click. It moves through a tiered approval chain sized to the amount, and the person who requests it can never be the one who approves or pays it out. Cash payouts are capped at ₦50,000 — anything larger must go out by bank transfer. Funds only actually leave the client's account balance at the final step, once every approval has been granted.

    Requested→Approved→Fully Approved→Disbursed

    Interest

    Interest-bearing Regular Savings accounts are credited automatically on the 1st of every month, based on the account's annual rate and its balance over the month. Smart Savings accounts instead earn a flat 6% every 3-month cycle, credited automatically the day the cycle matures — after which the cycle either renews or ends, depending on the client's preference. Breaking a Smart Savings cycle early (an early withdrawal) triggers an automatic penalty charged against the account instead.

    Chapter 05

    Cash & Banking

    Every naira the business holds — in a till, a safe, or a real bank account — has its own dedicated ledger account behind it.

    Bank accounts

    Each real-world bank account the business holds is set up once as a Bank Account record — account number, currency, an assigned manager, withdrawal limits — and Phoenix links it to its own dedicated GL account (accounts 1104 and up; 1101–1103 are reserved for Cash on Hand, Petty Cash and the branch's Main Bank). The account's displayed balance is always read live from that GL account, never stored separately, so it can never drift out of sync with the ledger.

    A bank account can be marked as dual-approval above a chosen threshold — a transfer over that amount needs a second, director-level sign-off before it goes out, and completion of that transfer pages the directors' Telegram group automatically either way.

    Cash on the floor: cashiers and tills

    Every teller, cashier or field agent who physically handles cash has their own cashier account — a live running cash balance backed by its own GL account, with an optional daily collection limit. At day's end, cash typically sweeps from a cashier's account into the branch's main bank account.

    Reconciliation

    Bank statements are matched against Phoenix's own recorded transactions automatically, but only a high-confidence match is ever posted without a person looking at it. Medium- or low-confidence matches are held out for manual review rather than auto-accepted — better an accountant spends two minutes confirming a match than the books silently absorb a wrong one. A director can always unmatch a bad auto-match later; the system keeps the original match history rather than erasing it, so nothing about a correction is lost.

    Why it's built this way

    A cautious, human-reviewed matching threshold is a deliberate trade-off — it means a small backlog of unmatched lines will always exist, in exchange for the guarantee that nothing gets auto-reconciled to the wrong transaction.

    Chapter 06

    Petty Cash

    A small, tightly controlled float for day-to-day branch spending — run as a classic imprest fund.

    The fund

    A Petty Cash Fund is a fixed float amount held by a named custodian (plus an alternate), with its own GL account and a per-transaction spending limit. When the float runs low, the custodian submits receipts for what's already been spent and requests a top-up (a replenishment) that restores the fund back to its fixed float — the top-up itself draws fresh cash out of the bank, it doesn't create any new expense on its own; the expense was already booked when each voucher was disbursed.

    Raising and paying a voucher

    Draft→Pending→Approved→Disbursed→Retired
  • Requestor raises a voucher — purpose, amount, payee, and an expense category — and submits it.
  • An approver reviews and approves it (must be a different person from the requestor when paying by bank transfer).
  • Only the fund's custodian, alternate custodian, or a director can disburse it. A bank-transfer payout additionally requires the disburser to be a third person, distinct from both requestor and approver.
  • The custodian later retires the voucher by submitting the actual receipt, recording any variance between what was requested and what was actually spent.

    • A voucher can cover several unrelated expenses at once — say, fuel and stationery in the same trip. Each line item carries its own expense category, so the posting splits correctly across each category's own expense account rather than lumping everything into one.

    📒 In the ledger — a two-line voucher disbursed in cash
    AccountDebitCredit
    Fuel Expense6,000
    Stationery Expense1,500
    Petty Cash Fund7,500

    If the fund's disbursement mode is set to bank transfer instead, the credit side hits the chosen bank account's GL account rather than the till, and the fund's own cash balance is untouched.

    Directors are watching

    Every petty cash disbursement — cash or bank transfer — fires an automatic Telegram alert to the directors' group the moment it happens. This isn't an approval gate, it's visibility: directors see every payout in near-real time without having to log in and check.

    Undoing a mistake

    A voucher can't simply be deleted once disbursed. Instead, someone requests a reversal, and a different authorised person must approve it before the original ledger entry is reversed and the fund's balance restored — the same maker-checker discipline applies to undoing a payment as to making one.

    Chapter 07

    Income & Expenses

    Money the business earns and spends outside of lending, savings and petty cash.

    Income

    Every income category is tied to its own income GL account — set that up once, and every income record filed under that category posts to the right place automatically. Where income is billed rather than collected on the spot, Phoenix raises an invoice: the amount owed is recorded straight away as a receivable, and only converts to cash later when it's actually collected. An invoice covering several kinds of income (say, registration plus a service fee) correctly splits across each item's own income account in one entry.

    📒 In the ledger — raising an invoice
    AccountDebitCredit
    Accounts Receivable15,000
    Service Fee Income15,000

    Loan interest and penalty income are handled directly inside the Loans module (Chapter 03) rather than here — this chapter's income tools are for everything else the business bills or earns.

    Expenses

    A general expense (as opposed to a petty cash voucher) records a category, amount, payee and payment method, and moves through the same shape of workflow: Draft → Submitted → Approved → Paid. Larger categories can be configured to require approval past a set threshold. Depending on whether it's paid immediately or the business is settling it later, it posts either straight against cash/bank or first into Accounts Payable.

    Office Use Requests

    When staff need something out of the store room for internal use — not for a client, not for resale — they raise an Office Use Request rather than a client-facing order. This replaced the old "Material Request" process, which used to be shared with client-facing requests; the two have now been kept fully separate.

    Draft→Submitted→Approved→Fulfilled

    On fulfilment, Phoenix checks stock at the chosen location, reduces it, and expenses the item automatically — using the expense account already configured against that item's category, not a manually picked account. See Chapter 08 for how stock itself is tracked.

    📒 In the ledger — fulfilling an office use request
    AccountDebitCredit
    Office Supplies Expense3,200
    Inventory — Stationery3,200

    Chapter 08

    Inventory & Procurement

    Buying stock in, tracking it per branch, and moving it between branches.

    Buying stock in

    Requisition→Purchase Order→Sent→Goods Received

    A staff member raises a Purchase Requisition, which — once approved — becomes a Purchase Order to a supplier. When the goods actually arrive, a Goods Received Note (GRN) is inspected and posted: this is the moment stock and the ledger both move, not when the order was merely placed.

    📒 In the ledger — posting a GRN on credit
    AccountDebitCredit
    Inventory — Stationery80,000
    Accounts Payable — Supplier80,000

    Every supplier has its own dedicated payable sub-ledger account, the same way every loan has its own receivable account — so at any moment you can see exactly what's owed to which supplier.

    Stock, per branch and per location

    Stock quantities are tracked per item, per specific location (a warehouse, store or vehicle) within a branch — never as one company-wide number. Every single change in quantity, whatever caused it, leaves behind a stock movement record, so a full history of every increase and decrease is always available.

    Moving stock between branches

    Approved→Dispatched→Acknowledged

    Dispatching a transfer reduces stock at the sending branch; acknowledging it (done by the receiving branch) adds it there — and if what arrives is less than what was sent, it's recorded as a short receipt rather than silently adjusted away. Moving stock within one branch touches no ledger accounts at all, since nothing has left the branch's books; moving it between branches posts through the same reciprocal clearing-account mechanism used for any interbranch transfer — see Chapter 09.

    Chapter 09

    Interbranch Transfers

    How money or stock crosses from one branch's books to another's without ever mixing the two.

    Why this needs its own mechanism

    Because every branch keeps its own separate chart of accounts, a single journal entry is never allowed to debit an account in one branch and credit an account in another. Instead, an interbranch transfer is really two ordinary, single-branch entries, linked together, each using a matching pair of "clearing" accounts: Due from [other branch] on one side, Due to [other branch] on the other.

    📒 In the ledger — Branch A sends ₦200,000 to Branch B

    Branch A's books:

    AccountDebitCredit
    Due from Branch B200,000
    Bank / Cash200,000

    Branch B's books:

    AccountDebitCredit
    Bank / Cash200,000
    Due to Branch A200,000

    Both legs post together, atomically — either the whole transfer goes through or none of it does — and can later be reversed as one linked pair. Because the two clearing accounts are always equal and opposite, adding them into a company-wide total would double count real money that never left the organisation; Chapter 10 explains how the consolidated trial balance cancels them out.

    Who can do this

    Creating and reversing an interbranch transfer is restricted to elevated, all-branch roles. A regular branch user can only ever view transfers that touch their own branch, as either the sender or the receiver.

    Chapter 10

    Reports

    Everything in this section is generated live from the ledger — nothing here is typed in separately.

    ReportWhat it tells you
    Trial BalanceEvery account and its balance as of a date, proving total debits equal total credits. Can be run summarised or in full detail.
    Consolidated Trial BalanceEvery branch's trial balance combined into one, with matching Due-from/Due-to interbranch pairs netted out so they don't double-count. Only available to all-branch roles.
    Profit & LossTotal income minus total expenses over a chosen period, optionally compared against a prior period.
    Balance SheetAssets, liabilities and equity as of a single date — what the business owns, owes, and is worth.
    Monthly Profit & LossOne row per income/expense account, one column per month — a spreadsheet-style view built for month-on-month comparison.
    Cash Flow StatementCash movement grouped into operating, investing and financing activity.

    Why the Trial Balance looks cleaner than the ledger

    Recall from Chapter 02 that every loan, savings account and till has its own individual sub-ledger account. Listing every one of those individually on a Trial Balance would bury the report in thousands of one-off rows — so the detailed Trial Balance hides these individual sub-ledger rows from view by default, while still counting every one of them correctly inside its parent account's total (e.g. "Loans Receivable"). Nothing is excluded from the arithmetic — only from the printed row.

    Chapter 11

    Roles, Approvals & Alerts

    Who can see what, who can approve how much, and who gets paged when something needs attention.

    The standard roles

    Every role combines a scope (whose records it can see) with an approval limit (the largest amount it can sign off on alone). This is the standard set of roles this business runs on:

    MD / CEO

    global · unlimited

    Operations Manager

    global · ₦10,000,000

    Branch Manager

    own branch · ₦5,000,000

    Loan Officer

    assigned clients · ₦500,000

    Recovery Officer

    own branch · no approval rights

    Teller / Cashier

    own branch · no approval rights

    Accountant

    own branch · full GL access, no approvals

    Auditor

    global · view & export only

    Data Entry Clerk

    own branch · create/edit only

    A user's actual permissions can also be individually extended or restricted beyond their role's defaults — for example, a temporary, time-limited grant to cover another branch while someone's on leave — and every action taken under such an extended grant is logged for audit.

    When a director gets paged

    A fixed set of events sends an automatic message to a directors'/branch-managers' Telegram group — not to one named person, but to the group, so any director on duty sees it:

    • Someone tries to approve a loan above their own approval limit.
    • A loan is disbursed.
    • A bank transfer exceeds an account's dual-approval threshold and needs a second sign-off, and again once that transfer completes.
    • A petty cash voucher is disbursed, cash or bank transfer.

    A failed Telegram send is never allowed to block the underlying approval or payment it's reporting on — the alert quietly retries in the background, and the action it's describing has already gone through regardless.

    Chapter 12

    Glossary

    Plain-English definitions of every accounting term used in this manual.

    TermMeaning
    Debit / CreditThe two sides of every ledger entry. Which one increases a balance depends on the account type — see Chapter 02.
    Chart of AccountsThe full numbered list of every account a branch's books are built from, grouped into Assets, Liabilities, Equity, Income and Expenses.
    General Ledger (GL)The complete record of every posted transaction across every account — the books, in full.
    Journal entry / TransactionOne balanced posting: two or more lines, debits equal to credits, hitting one or more accounts.
    Sub-ledgerAn individual account auto-linked to one specific loan, savings account, till or supplier, rolling up into its parent account's total.
    Trial BalanceEvery account and its balance as of a date, with total debits proven equal to total credits.
    Balance SheetWhat the business owns, owes and is worth, at one point in time.
    Profit & Loss (P&L)Income minus expenses, over a period of time.
    ReconciliationMatching a bank's own statement against what the system recorded, to catch anything missing or wrong on either side.
    DisbursementPaying money out — most often, releasing an approved loan to a client.
    Maker-checkerA control requiring the person who requests an action and the person who approves (and often pays) it to be two different people.
    Branch scopingRestricting what a user can see or do to their own branch's records, unless their role grants access across all branches.
    Due from / Due toThe matched pair of clearing accounts used to record money or stock crossing between two branches — see Chapter 09.
    Consolidated Trial BalanceEvery branch's books combined into one, with interbranch Due-from/Due-to balances cancelled out.
    Imprest fundA fixed cash float (like Petty Cash) that's topped back up to its original amount each time it's replenished, rather than re-funded from scratch.
    Accounts Payable / ReceivableMoney the business owes to suppliers (Payable) or is owed by others (Receivable), before cash actually changes hands.
